# Hardcore mode: Implement reallocate() without calling realloc(), malloc(), or free(). You are allowed to call malloc() once, at the beginning of the interpreter’s execution, to allocate a single big block of memory, which your reallocate() function has access to. It parcels out blobs of memory from that single region, your own personal heap. It’s your job to define how it does that.

Dynamic memory allocation in C: malloc() and free() are just functions that manage a region of memory called the heap. Most implementations use a combination of metadata and free lists to keep track of which parts of the heap are in use and which parts are available. Each allocated block usually has a small header containing its size and whether it’s free or not. Free blocks are often linked together in a list so the allocator can quickly find a block of the right size.
When you call malloc(), the allocator searches its free lists for a block large enough to satisfy the request. If it finds one, it may split the block into a used portion and a smaller free remainder. When you call free(), the allocator marks the block as free and tries to merge it with neighboring free blocks to reduce fragmentation. More advanced allocators use size‑segregated bins, buddy systems, or slab allocators to make allocation faster and reduce fragmentation.
