# Our VM’s stack has a fixed size, and we don’t check if pushing a value overflows it. This means the wrong series of instructions could cause our interpreter to crash or go into undefined behavior. Avoid that by dynamically growing the stack as needed.
# What are the costs and benefits of doing so?

The VM uses a fixed‑size stack, and it doesn’t check for overflow. That means a deeply nested expression or a weird sequence of bytecode could push more values than the stack can hold, which leads to memory corruption or a crash. The simplest fix is to make the stack grow dynamically, just like we do with arrays or the chunk’s bytecode. The benefit: the VM becomes much safer. Any program, even one with extremely deep recursion or huge expressions, will keep working as long as the machine has memory. It also makes the interpreter more robust against accidental or malicious bytecode. The downside is performance and complexity. Every push now needs to check whether the stack is full, and if it is, we have to allocate a bigger array and copy the old contents over. That’s not something you want happening in the middle of a tight loop. It also means more bookkeeping and slightly more memory overhead. In practice, though, stack growth happens rarely, so the cost is usually small compared to the safety you gain. Overall, dynamically growing the stack trades a tiny bit of speed for correctness and stability, which is almost always worth it in a real interpreter.
